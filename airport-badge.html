How it updates

  • Each time someone opens your page, the badge shows the latest METAR for your field (from aviationweather.gov), cached for up to 10 minutes.
  • The iframe card also refreshes itself every 15 minutes while it's on screen.
  • No METAR at your field? It uses the nearest METAR within 25 nm, adjusts the temperature for any significant difference in elevation, and shows that station's ID and time.
  • Nothing within 25 nm? It falls back to a weather-model estimate and labels it "model est." on the badge.
  • Observations more than three hours old are skipped, and if no weather is available the badge says "Unavailable right now" rather than showing a stale number.

Why put density altitude on your site

Pilots already know the number matters. The badge puts it where they look before they come.

Safety

The number behind many hot-day mishaps

High density altitude is a well-known factor in takeoff and climb accidents at high-elevation airports, especially on summer afternoons. FAA guidance, including the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, tells pilots to account for it before every high, hot departure.

Visitors

Transient pilots check your site first

Visiting pilots look up your runways, fuel and services before they fly in. A live density altitude right there tells a flatland pilot what your field will feel like today, and nudges them toward a cooler morning departure.

Frequently asked questions

Is the badge really free?

Yes. There's no signup, no account and no API key, and there are no ads in the badge. Copy the code, paste it into your page, and you're done.

How current is the density altitude?

It's computed from the latest METAR for your field and cached for up to 10 minutes. Routine METARs are issued every hour, with extra reports when the weather changes, and the badge prints the observation time in Zulu so pilots can see exactly how fresh it is.

What if my airport has no METAR?

The badge uses the nearest METAR within 25 nm, adjusts its temperature for any significant difference in elevation, and shows that station's ID and time so pilots know where the numbers come from. If there's no reporting station within 25 nm, it uses a weather-model estimate and says "model est." on the badge.

Will it slow down my website?

No. The image badge is a plain SVG of about 1–2 KB, served from a CDN and cached. It runs no scripts and sets no cookies on your site. The iframe card is a tiny self-contained page that loads lazily.

Can I change the size or colours?

Pick the dark or light style above (the light one adds ?theme=light to the image address). The badge is a vector image, so you can scale it by changing the width and height attributes in proportion, for example width="450" height="108", and it stays sharp. The coloured bar follows the conditions (green, amber or red) and can't be changed.

Can pilots use the badge for flight planning?

No. It's an educational awareness tool that helps pilots notice when density altitude is high. For go/no-go decisions, pilots should use their aircraft's POH performance charts and official weather from a METAR, ATIS/AWOS or a standard briefing.

How the numbers are computed

Atmosphere — International Standard Atmosphere (ISA): the barometric formula in the troposphere (1.98 °C / 1000 ft lapse) and the isothermal layer above the tropopause (~36,089 ft).
Blood oxygen — alveolar gas equation P_AO₂ = 0.2095·(P − 47) − P_CO₂/0.8 feeding the Severinghaus SpO₂ curve. CO₂ falls with altitude, tuned to acute-exposure data (~87% @ 10k, ~72% @ 18k, ~50% @ 25k ft).
Density altitude — PA = elev + (29.92 − altimeter)·1000, then DA = PA + 120·(OAT − ISA temp).
Horsepower — Gagg–Ferrar relation HP ≈ rated·(1.132σ − 0.132), σ = density ratio at your density altitude; turbos hold rated power to a critical altitude.
Power curve — parabolic drag polar C_D = C_D0 + C_L²/(πAR·e); power required = drag × TAS. The region of reversed command is the band from stall up to the minimum-power speed. Drag values are representative estimates and V-speeds (Vs, Va, Vno, Vne) are typical published figures — check your POH; the climb readout is idealized (constant ~80% prop efficiency) and optimistic at low speed.
Cabin altitude — cabin_P = min(sea level, ambient + ΔP) inverted to an altitude; ΔP is each aircraft's published max pressure differential.
Data sources — bundled ~110 airports (offline elevation) plus aviationweather.gov METAR for live temp/altimeter (via a same-origin proxy once hosted), falling back to open-meteo.com current weather by coordinates for fields without a METAR.
References — FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge (FAA-H-8083-25) and Airplane Flying Handbook (FAA-H-8083-3) for density altitude, the FAR 91.211 oxygen rules, and the power curve / region of reversed command; J.D. Anderson, Introduction to Flight (parabolic drag polar, power-required = drag × TAS); the alveolar gas equation and Severinghaus SpO₂ relation, with acute-exposure SaO₂ figures from high-altitude physiology literature (e.g. J.B. West). Power-curve V-speeds and the narrow trainer reversed-command band cross-checked against Van's Air Force, GoFly, and Boldmethod.

Educational models for healthy unacclimatized adults at acute exposure. Real SpO₂ varies with fitness, acclimatization, and individual physiology. Not for medical or operational flight-planning use.
