    Method & caveats

    Live board

    Each airport's latest METAR from aviationweather.gov supplies the temperature and altimeter setting. Combined with field elevation, density altitude comes from the same formula as the density altitude calculator:

    1 · Pressure altitudePA = elevation + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1000
    2 · ISA temperatureISA temp = 15 − 1.98 × PA / 1000 (°C)
    3 · Density altitudeDA = PA + 120 × (OAT − ISA temp)

    vs field is density altitude minus field elevation — how much higher the airplane thinks it is than the runway it's sitting on. It's negative on a cold, high-pressure day. Colors: green below +1,000 ft, amber +1,000 to +2,999 ft, red +3,000 ft or more.

    Summer ranking

    Summer DA = field elevation + 120 × (mean July daily high − ISA temperature). The July high is the 2024–25 mean daily maximum from the ERA5 reanalysis (via Open-Meteo), taken at field elevation. It assumes standard pressure (29.92 inHg) and ignores humidity, so it's a ranking of how hot-and-high each field typically runs — real afternoons vary by ±1,000 ft or more.

    Which airports

    Airports were screened from FAA NASR data (October 2026 cycle): public-use, operational, with fuel available. The live board tracks the roughly 300 of these that report a METAR and have a typical summer-afternoon density altitude of 4,500 ft or more.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which US airport has the highest density altitude?

    Lake County Airport (KLXV) in Leadville, Colorado. At 9,934 ft it's the highest public-use airport in North America, and on a typical July afternoon its density altitude is close to 13,000 ft. Telluride (KTEX) and Creede (C24) follow at roughly 12,200 and 11,800 ft. Leadville usually tops the live board too, but a lower desert field on a hot afternoon can post the biggest jump above its own elevation.

    Why is density altitude highest on summer afternoons?

    Temperature is the big day-to-day driver. Every 1 °C above standard adds roughly 120 ft of density altitude, and surface temperature peaks in mid-to-late afternoon. A 7,000 ft field at 30 °C is about 29 °C warmer than standard — roughly 3,500 ft of extra density altitude — while the same field at dawn may sit only a few hundred feet above its elevation. Low pressure ahead of storms adds more. That's why mountain pilots like to depart early.

    How much does density altitude hurt takeoff performance?

    As a rule of thumb, a normally-aspirated light single needs roughly 10% more takeoff distance for every 1,000 ft of density altitude and loses about 3% of its engine power per 1,000 ft. At 8,000 ft density altitude that's about a quarter less power and a ground roll approaching double the sea-level figure, with climb rate hurt even more. These are rules of thumb only — your POH performance charts govern. Try the takeoff and landing distance calculator to see the effect.

    How often is the Hot & High board updated?

    The board pulls the latest METAR for every airport and refreshes about every 10 minutes; leave the page open and it updates itself. Most airports issue a METAR once an hour, plus specials when the weather changes, so each row shows its observation time in Zulu and how old it is. The summer ranking is a climatology and changes only when it's re-run with new data.

    Why isn't my airport on the live board?

    The live board tracks about 300 METAR-reporting US airports whose typical summer-afternoon density altitude is 4,500 ft or more. Airports without their own weather report — several on the summer list, such as Creede (C24) — can't be shown live. You can still check any airport in the density altitude calculator, which uses the nearest METAR within 25 nm.

    How the numbers are computed

    Atmosphere — International Standard Atmosphere (ISA): the barometric formula in the troposphere (1.98 °C / 1000 ft lapse) and the isothermal layer above the tropopause (~36,089 ft).
    Blood oxygen — alveolar gas equation P_AO₂ = 0.2095·(P − 47) − P_CO₂/0.8 feeding the Severinghaus SpO₂ curve. CO₂ falls with altitude, tuned to acute-exposure data (~87% @ 10k, ~72% @ 18k, ~50% @ 25k ft).
    Density altitude — PA = elev + (29.92 − altimeter)·1000, then DA = PA + 120·(OAT − ISA temp).
    Horsepower — Gagg–Ferrar relation HP ≈ rated·(1.132σ − 0.132), σ = density ratio at your density altitude; turbos hold rated power to a critical altitude.
    Power curve — parabolic drag polar C_D = C_D0 + C_L²/(πAR·e); power required = drag × TAS. The region of reversed command is the band from stall up to the minimum-power speed. Drag values are representative estimates and V-speeds (Vs, Va, Vno, Vne) are typical published figures — check your POH; the climb readout is idealized (constant ~80% prop efficiency) and optimistic at low speed.
    Cabin altitude — cabin_P = min(sea level, ambient + ΔP) inverted to an altitude; ΔP is each aircraft's published max pressure differential.
    Data sources — bundled ~110 airports (offline elevation) plus aviationweather.gov METAR for live temp/altimeter (via a same-origin proxy once hosted), falling back to open-meteo.com current weather by coordinates for fields without a METAR.
    References — FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge (FAA-H-8083-25) and Airplane Flying Handbook (FAA-H-8083-3) for density altitude, the FAR 91.211 oxygen rules, and the power curve / region of reversed command; J.D. Anderson, Introduction to Flight (parabolic drag polar, power-required = drag × TAS); the alveolar gas equation and Severinghaus SpO₂ relation, with acute-exposure SaO₂ figures from high-altitude physiology literature (e.g. J.B. West). Power-curve V-speeds and the narrow trainer reversed-command band cross-checked against Van's Air Force, GoFly, and Boldmethod.

    Educational models for healthy unacclimatized adults at acute exposure. Real SpO₂ varies with fitness, acclimatization, and individual physiology. Not for medical or operational flight-planning use.
